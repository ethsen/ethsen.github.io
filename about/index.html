Who I am

A short summary about my work, my interests, and the things that keep me curious.

About

I am a graduate student at the University of Pennsylvania pursuing an M.S.E. in Robotics. I previously earned a B.A. in Computational Physics from Franklin & Marshall College, where I developed a rigorous foundation in mathematics, physics, and computation. My academic and professional interests lie at the intersection of robotics, computer vision, and deep learning, with a particular focus on state estimation and decision-making in complex systems.

I am most drawn to technical problems that require multi-step reasoning, careful attention to both theory and implementation, and a willingness to engage deeply with detail. Over time, I have worked on a range of problems including fashion-focused computer vision models, soccer analytics and reinforcement learning frameworks, drone path planning and flight optimization, and six-degree-of-freedom robotic manipulation. While these projects span different domains, they are united by a common goal: using perception and learning to make better decisions under uncertainty.

What differentiates me most is breadth, not just across technical tools, but across ways of thinking. I am comfortable moving between theory and practice, between individual contribution and team collaboration. My background in computational physics laid a rigorous foundation, but the broader liberal arts environment at Franklin & Marshall shaped how I communicate, collaborate, and think about the impact of technical work. I do not see engineering as something that exists in isolation from culture, people, or context.

Research interests and personal projects

Alongside formal coursework, I approach personal projects as both a portfolio and a space for exploration. Rather than focusing exclusively on robotics, I have intentionally applied similar technical ideas to domains that feel intellectually engaging and personally meaningful, particularly soccer and fashion.

In computer vision, my fashion-focused projects explore how deep learning models interpret visual structure, style, and texture, probing what these systems learn beyond surface-level correlations. In sports analytics, my soccer projects include reinforcement learning formulations and pass forecasting models designed to capture decision-making in a dynamic, multi-agent environment. These projects emphasize structured spatial representations and long-horizon reasoning, reflecting the complexity of real decision processes in sport. Within robotics, I have gravitated toward systems-level work, including implementing full pipelines for visual–inertial odometry, motion planning, trajectory generation, and control for autonomous navigation.

A recurring question across all of this work is interpretability. I care deeply about how models reason, not just how well they perform. As machine learning systems increasingly inform real decisions made by non-experts, I believe it is important to design architectures and training strategies that provide insight into what a model sees and why it behaves the way it does. This perspective shapes both the problems I choose to work on and the criteria by which I evaluate success.

Perspective beyond the work

Long before robotics or machine learning became central to my life, soccer played that role. Competing as a collegiate athlete alongside a demanding academic program shaped how I approach long-term challenges. Progress was often incremental, outcomes uncertain, and failure a frequent teacher. Over time, I experienced a wide range of roles, from starter to bench player to team captain, each offering lessons in leadership, communication, and resilience. Those experiences continue to influence how I work today, reinforcing the value of patience, discipline, and sustained effort when progress is not immediately visible.

My relationship with literature, and more broadly with how to think and imagine, was profoundly shaped by Professor Scott Lerner at Franklin & Marshall College, the most influential professor of my life. Through his courses on Italo Calvino and Luigi Pirandello, he taught me how to read carefully, how to write with intention, and how to approach texts as sources of insight rather than objects of analysis alone. Works such as Il sentiero dei nidi di ragno and Il fu Mattia Pascal explore identity, ambiguity, and the tension between structure and freedom, themes that resonated deeply through sustained reading and reflection. His teaching extended far beyond the classroom, shaping how I understand art, how I interpret ambiguity, and how I think about meaning and context. Although his courses were not technical, they fundamentally changed how I approach the world and, by extension, the work I do today.

At my core, I am guided by a mindset shaped by athletics, liberal arts education, and technical practice. I believe that breadth, an ability to see context, history, and consequence, allows one to approach problems differently than a purely technical perspective alone. Engineering and machine learning do not exist in isolation; they are embedded within social, cultural, and human systems, and their impacts extend far beyond performance metrics. Seeing the context in which products and models exist is central to meaningful progress, and being blind to those contexts is often what leads to harm or poor decision-making. My work is driven by the belief that thoughtful engineering, grounded in both rigor and perspective, is essential to building systems that genuinely serve people.
