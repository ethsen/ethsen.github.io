I am a graduate student at the University of Pennsylvania pursuing an M.S.E. in Robotics. I previously earned a B.A. in Computational Physics from Franklin & Marshall College where this experience laid formed my technical foundation, while the broader liberal arts environment at Franklin & Marshall shaped how I communicate, collaborate, and think about the impact of technical work. My academic and professional interests lie at the intersection of robotics, computer vision, and deep learning, with a particular focus on state estimation and decision-making in complex systems.

I am most drawn to technical problems that require careful attention to both theory and implementation, requiring a willingness to engage deeply with detail. Over time, I have worked on a range of problems including fashion-focused computer vision models, soccer analytics and reinforcement learning frameworks, drone path planning and flight optimization, and six DoF robotic manipulation. While these projects span different domains, they are united by the common goal of using perception and learning to make better decisions under uncertainty.


Alongside formal coursework, I approach personal projects as both a portfolio and a space for exploration. Rather than focusing exclusively on robotics, I have intentionally applied similar technical ideas to domains that feel intellectually engaging and personally meaningful, particularly soccer and fashion. These projects are motivated less by the domains themselves and more by a recurring interest in how learning-based systems form internal representations of the world.

One of the ideas that has most strongly shaped my recent work is representation learning, particularly as described in Antonio Torralba’s Foundations of Computer Vision. That perspective fundamentally changed how I think about deep learning models. In essence, a model’s understanding of the world is not intrinsic; it is shaped by the objective it is trained to optimize. The loss function determines what the model attends to, how it compresses information, and which aspects of the data it ultimately finds meaningful. As Torralba succinctly states, “A good representation is one that makes subsequent problem solving easier.”

I often think about this process through a simple analogy. Imagine a toddler who is rewarded every time they correctly identify a cardinal in the sky. At first, the bird appears as a complex combination of color, shape, texture, and motion. Over time, driven by the incentive, the child no longer needs to process the full richness of the scene. The concept of a cardinal becomes reduced to a few salient cues: red coloration, a triangular silhouette, a particular pattern of movement. Recognition becomes efficient because the internal representation has become structured. The child no longer sees the entire bird; they see just enough.

This is precisely how learning-based models operate. Whether in vision, language, or control, models learn to compress reality into representations that are useful for the task they are given. When we ask a generative model to produce an image of a dog, it does not retrieve a stored picture, but instead draws from an internal representation encoding features such as shape, texture, posture, and variation. In this sense, we do not simply train models to perform tasks; we train them to perceive. The objectives we define implicitly determine how a model comes to understand the world.

From this perspective, model behavior reflects our own assumptions as much as it reflects the system itself. Biases, blind spots, and failure modes are often consequences of the objectives and constraints we impose rather than inherent flaws of the model. This becomes especially apparent in domains such as art, fashion, music, and sport, where meaning, creativity, and decision-making are inherently high-dimensional and resistant to clean formalization. When models struggle in these settings, they reveal not only the limits of their representations, but also the limits of how we have chosen to frame the problem.

As engineers and researchers, we are responsible for understanding how our systems interpret the data we present to them, and for being honest about what those interpretations exclude. Careful inspection of learned representations is essential for both objective and subjective evaluation, particularly when models are applied outside traditional engineering domains and into human-centered contexts. This belief motivates both my interest in interpretability and my choice to work across non-traditional domains. By examining how models compress and reason about complex systems, we place ourselves in a better position to judge whether they are truly accomplishing what we intend them to do.

I do not see engineering as something that exists in isolation from culture, people, or context.
