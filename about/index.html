Perspective beyond the work

Long before robotics or machine learning became central to my life, soccer occupied that role. I was given the opportunity to continue this passion into college, where soccer offered its final and perhaps most important lessons. The constant rhythm of switching focus between competing as an athlete and a demanding academic program shaped how I approach problems today.

The other half of my focus was dedicated to pursuing my degree in computational physics and applied math. In all honesty, I was never the greatest physicist, but I deeply appreciated how physics attempts to define reality. Above all else, physics laid my technical foundation, which provided me a toolset to be able to interrogate other topics, specifically Robotics/Machine Learning.Equally formative in shaping my foundation was my experience studying Italian literature under Professor Scott Lerner at F&M. Professor Lerner's courses acted as a healthy escape from the very objective world of math and physics. I learned to sit with ambiguity and uncertainty far differently than how it would be handled in a technical domain.

Finally, pursuing my Masters in Robotics has served as the most important layer in my foundation. I cannot speak more highly of the professors and peers I have had the chance to engage with; through learning from them, I sit here a fundamentally changed man. Taking vision courses taught by Professors Kostas Daniilidis and Jianbo Shi, shaped my passion and understanding of computer vision. While Professor Pratik Chaudhari's courses on learning laid my foundation on how to create systems that learn.

Research interests and personal projects

This section includes some of my favorite paintings.

The topic that has most strongly influenced my recent work is compressive representation learning methods, as described by Antonio Torralba in Foundations of Computer Vision. Fundamentally, he lays out the central representation learning objective as mapping datapoints to an abstract space, or in essence, a new representation. He notes that, “A good representation is one that makes subsequent problem solving easier.” Viewed this way, compressive representation learning is a process of structured abstraction, where high-dimensional data is reduced into representations that preserve what matters for a given goal. Thinking about learning in these terms provides a concrete way to reason about how models interpret data, and why different objectives can lead to fundamentally different internal representations.

I often think about this process through a simple analogy. Imagine a toddler who is rewarded every time they correctly identify a cardinal in the sky. At first, the bird appears as a complex combination of color, shape, texture, and motion. Over time, driven by the incentive, the child no longer needs to process the full richness of the scene. The concept of a cardinal becomes reduced to a few salient cues: red coloration, a triangular silhouette, a particular pattern of movement. Recognition becomes efficient because the internal representation has become structured. The child no longer sees the entire bird; they see just enough.

This is why I find machine learning projects in non-traditional domains so compelling. My interest in areas such as art, fashion, and sport comes from a belief that representation learning is most revealing when applied to systems that resist compression. In these settings, performance alone is insufficient; understanding how a model interprets the data becomes essential to evaluating both its limitations and the assumptions embedded in its design. Model behavior, from this perspective, reflects our objectives as much as it reflects the system itself, since the objectives we define encode what we consider meaningful. When models struggle in these domains, they often expose mismatches between our formalizations and the underlying complexity of the problem. The same lens applies to more traditional fields such as robotics, where learned representations directly shape perception, planning, and control, and where failure modes can have tangible real-world consequences. Across both settings, careful inspection of representations is not optional, but central to understanding whether a system has learned something faithful to the task we intend it to perform.

In these settings, representation learning reveals both the power and the limitations of machine learning. Models attempt to compress vast, nuanced human systems into tractable internal structures, and in doing so, they expose what is lost as much as what is retained. When that compression is carefully structured, abstraction can still preserve meaning, but only if we understand what has been emphasized and what has been discarded. For me, this makes interpretability a first-order requirement: not merely a way to explain a prediction after the fact, but a means of assessing whether a system has learned something faithful or merely something useful. The goal is to build models that serve a purpose while remaining legible enough to evaluate honestly, especially when the domain itself cannot be reduced to a single tidy definition of correctness.

What inspired this page design?

The initial inspiration for the design of my page came from a photography series by Zili Zhang that I saw at the Rittenhouse Square Fine Art Show in Philadelphia, specifically his White and Blue series. I appreciated the simplicity of his work and specifically the color palette he captured.

In particular, the deep blue seen in the photo White and Blue No.6 reminded me of Yves Klein’s Blue Monochrome painting, which I saw at the MoMa. The painting itself captures a single deep blue color covering the entire canvas, which stood out to me when I first saw it in person. The depth of the blue is what I loved the most, and in many ways you kind of get lost in it if you just sit and stare for a while. It may seem odd, but if you have the chance to see it in person, I encourage you to do the same. Inevitably, I ended up picking Klein Blue as the main color for my page, which led me to thinking about how I could incorporate it into a design/theme for the page.

I knew I wanted to keep the design simple and, if possible, abstract in some way. This led me to a rather unique source of inspiration being the SpongeBob episode “SB-129,” specifically the scene where Squidward is transported into the nowhere dimension. It is one of my favorite episodes, and the abstract geometric space that is the nowhere dimension felt like the perfect way to combine the color pallete with a simple abstract design.

I ended up writing a basic script to make the backgrounds of the page using matplotlib, which I would be happy to share if you are curious. I want to close this by saying that I know nothing about design principles, and know very little about art. However, since this is my website, it is in many ways an expression of myself and I wanted to explain how I came about the design. If you're reading this, you were at the very least curious to find out, and I appreciate that, as you got to know me a little better :)
